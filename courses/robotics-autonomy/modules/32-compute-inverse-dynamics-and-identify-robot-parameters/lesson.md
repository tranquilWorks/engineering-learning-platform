# Identify Payload and Damping from Synthetic Joint Torque



## Model, derivation, and conventions

`tau=0.08 qddot + [0.25 qddot+4.905 cos(q), qdot] theta`

`theta_hat=argmin ||Y theta-y||²+0.02||theta-theta_prior||²`

`heldout_error=tau_predicted-tau_true`

This experiment identifies two parameters of a single ideal revolute joint: an equivalent distal payload mass in kilograms and viscous damping in N m s/rad. The known rotor inertia is 0.08 kg m². The payload acts at a 0.5 m lever, so its inertial coefficient is 0.25*qddot and its gravity coefficient is 4.905*cos(q). The true synthetic parameter vector is [1,0.12]. All training and validation data are generated by declared equations; these are not measurements from a real robot.

Normal training uses q=0.4+A*(sin(1.3t)+0.25sin(3.1t)) over six seconds at 121 samples. Velocity and acceleration are analytic derivatives, avoiding a hidden numerical differentiation filter. The amplitude slider changes A and therefore changes excitation. Subtract the known rotor term from measured torque to get the regression target. The two columns of Y are the mass coefficient and joint velocity. A deterministic disturbance 0.015*sin(2.7t+0.2) N m is added to training torque, so even a correct model need not fit every sample exactly.

The selected payload guess enters a prior [guess,0.08], not the synthetic ground truth. The objective adds a ridge weight 0.02 after normalizing each parameter by one unit of its stated engineering unit and each torque residual by one N m. This convention makes the written dimensionless least-squares objective meaningful despite the different parameter units. The production fit solves an augmented least-squares system containing Y and the square root of the ridge weight times identity. Regularization makes the numerical solution unique even when the data cannot identify both parameters; it does not create missing information.

The condition metric is the singular-value condition number of that regularized augmented design, not the condition number of the unregularized physical regressor. Raw regressor rank is retained separately. In the faulty experiment, q stays at 0.4 rad and both derivatives are zero. The damping column is exactly zero, making raw rank one. The fitted damping then remains its prior value, 0.08, regardless of how many repeated stationary samples are collected.

Validation uses a separate trajectory q=0.3+0.45*cos(1.7t), with analytic derivatives and noiseless true torque. It reports torque RMS error, fitted payload error and regularized condition. This separation prevents a small training residual from being mistaken for predictive validity. The validation trajectory excites damping, so the stationary-training defect becomes observable in its residual curve. A favorable single validation trajectory still does not establish identifiability under arbitrary experiments or transfer to physical hardware.

## Two one-variable sweeps

1. Change the payload prior while holding excitation fixed. Compare fitted payload and held-out torque error. Strong data should reduce prior influence, but the ridge term means the result is not generally independent of the guess.

2. Increase excitation amplitude at fixed prior. Inspect both regressor columns, fitted parameters and held-out residuals. In faulty stationary mode the amplitude slider no longer supplies dynamic excitation.

Return to defaults between sweeps. Hold the other control fixed and record both a changing output and an expected invariant. Explain the physical or numerical path from the selected input to the observed response.

## Recovery

Restore the multisine and compare raw rank, fitted damping and held-out torque error. Do not repair the result by changing the held-out truth or labeling a finite regularized condition as full identifiability.

Repeat a saved nominal setting and confirm that its values and curves return. Recovery must restore the governing mechanism and its evidence, not merely clear a warning.

## Alternative and limiting cases

Stationary samples cannot identify viscous damping because velocity is zero. The ridge solution depends on the stated units and prior; real identification also requires sensor calibration, suitable noise assumptions and a model of unmodeled dynamics.

## Common mistakes

Do not infer correctness from a changing headline alone. Stationary samples cannot identify viscous damping because velocity is zero.

Do not change both sliders at once and attribute the result to one cause. Separate a model assumption from a measured property, and a finite-horizon observation from a universal guarantee. Floating-point roundoff is not a physical effect; equally, an attractive plot is not a substitute for the governing calculation.
